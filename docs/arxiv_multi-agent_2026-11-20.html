Coordinated Offloading: Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for the Edge-Cloud Continuum

Authors:Muhammad Rafid, Golshan Famitafreshi, Vasilis Avgerinos, Kostas Ramantas, Christos Verikoukis
Date:2026-11-19 11:18:55

The increasing demand for intelligent, low-latency services in edge-cloud continuum systems poses new challenges for dynamic and efficient task offloading. We propose a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework for distributed task offloading under partial observability, where each device offloads only a portion of its computation to a target edge server while executing the remaining portion locally (partial offloading). The problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where each device independently learns an optimal policy under partially observable system states. To address the non-convexity, high-dimensional state space, and continuous action space, we develop a multi-agent Advantage-Weighted Actor-Critic (AWAC)-enhanced Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)-based computation offloading algorithm. The proposed method combines the stable policy updates of PPO with an advantage-weighting mechanism inspired by AWAC to emphasize higher-quality actions during training. To encourage cooperation without inter-device communication, a shared global reward aligned with system cost is employed. Extensive simulations demonstrate rapid convergence and significant improvement in overall system cost compared with baselines, e.g., Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) and Deep Q-Network (DQN).

In this paper, we propose a personalized privacy-aware task offloading framework, where the privacy requirement of each task is quantified through a User-Centric Privacy (UCP) model that considers task sensitivity, user privacy preferences, and task urgency. Addressing Exogenous Variability in Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Authors:Seongmin Kim, Woohyeon Byeon, Jiwon Jeon, Seungyul Han, Youngchul Sung
Date:2026-11-17 01:43:42

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) often fails under exogenous variability such as shifts in opponents or environment regimes that cannot be controlled by the team but alter the transition dynamics. We formalize this challenge as Exogenous Dec-POMDP (ED-POMDP), in which an episode-level exogenous regime, selected independently of the team, indexes the dynamics, and the global state is partitioned into components that are directly responsive to the current team action and components that are not directly action-responsive at the current step. This formulation exposes mediated influence: while team actions do not directly update the latter at the current step, they can shape their later evolution through induced changes in the former. Based on this, we propose LEICA, a CTDE-compatible algorithm that learns history-conditioned context representations for both components and shapes policy updates using agent-wise, influence-weighted counterfactual intrinsic rewards. Across SMAX benchmarks with opponent strategy shifts, LEICA consistently improves both training performance and generalization to unseen opponents' strategies over existing baselines, supporting the usefulness of influence-weighted shaping under exogenous train-test regime shifts.

Authors:Dominic Klukas, Cyrus Neary
Date:2026-11-14 01:27:53

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problems for heterogeneous teams require agents to collaborate to complete team tasks while making the best use of diverse agent capabilities. We introduce assume/guarantee contracts for heterogeneous multi-agent systems (AGC-MAS) and Iterative Heterogeneous Assignment for MARL (IHA-MARL). AGC-MAS contracts are compositional objects that encode the assumptions provided to, and the guarantees required of, each subtask contributing to the overall team task. We formulate contracts as tuples that specify a subtask, an automaton, timeout and success-probability requirements, and dispatch behavior. The automaton tracks an agent's high-level progress through the subtask associated with its contract and provides a natural mechanism for synchronization across contracts. Our abstraction enables robust interaction across heterogeneous low-level subtask implementations, allows heterogeneous agents to substitute for one another in satisfying contracts, and supports reuse of previously satisfied contracts in new team contexts. Having defined our theoretical framework, we develop a decomposition algorithm that breaks down a team task into a set of contracts. We then propose an iterative algorithm for task assignment and MARL that alternates between assigning contracts representing subtasks to individual agents, training the assigned agents to satisfy their contracts, and assessing their resulting capabilities to inform the next round of task assignments until the team's success probability meets the desired threshold. Our experimental results demonstrate the versatility of contracts in enabling agent substitution and contract reuse. Across three benchmarks with five seeds each, IHA-MARL achieves observed $100\%$ final evaluation success. In CPU timing pilots, comparable baselines required approximately $7$--$11\times$ more training-loop time.

Imitation Bootstrapped Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Authors:Tony H. Zhang, Connor Mattson, Daniel S. Brown
Date:2026-11-13 18:50:05

Demonstrations are an effective way to accelerate single-agent reinforcement learning (RL), but extending this idea to multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is non-trivial as it is difficult and often impossible for a single person to teleoperate multiple agents simultaneously. Round-Robin Behavior Cloning (R2BC) addresses this by letting a single demonstrator control one agent at a time while the others execute their current policies. However, the resulting imitation policies are limited by the quality of the demonstrations. We propose Imitation Bootstrapped Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (IBMARL), a framework that extends Imitation Bootstrapped Reinforcement Learning (IBRL) to cooperative teams and uses R2BC to train the imitation policies. At each time step, every agent receives one action proposal from its imitation policy and one from its RL policy, and an ensemble of centralized critics selects among the per-agent combinations of the two. A gated imitation penalty, a soft Q-filtered behavior cloning loss, regularizes each RL policy toward its imitation policy only where the critics value the imitation action more, so that after training each agent executes its RL policy alone, without the centralized critics. On four cooperative multi-agent RL benchmarks, IBMARL surpasses its imitation policies $2.6$-$4.2\times$ faster than demonstration-based baselines on two tasks, is the only method to surpass it on the other two, and exceeds the demonstrator itself on two of the four. With real human demonstrations IBMARL still surpasses imitation learning alone, and does so more than twice as fast as the best baseline.

Date:2026-11-06 15:57:18

We study cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problems in which agents evolve under decoupled noisy dynamics but share a population objective. A canonical example is a mobility operator that orchestrates a fleet of vehicles to meet customer demand. The stochastic and time-varying nature of these problems calls for learning approaches, yet existing MARL algorithms do not scale: learning assignment and routing jointly over the full state-action space becomes prohibitive as the number of agents grows. Formulating the problem over the space of probability measures, we prove a separation principle: the population cost-to-go function is upper bounded by an optimal transport problem whose transportation cost is the target-conditioned single-agent cost-to-go function. This simplifies the multi-agent problem to learning a single-agent policy and coordinating the agents via optimal transport. Building on this principle, we introduce SALT (Separation-based Assignment and Learning via optimal Transport), a family of algorithms that learn a target-conditioned single-agent policy using any standard reinforcement learning algorithm, and then coordinate the agents via optimal transport. Since learning occurs at the single-agent level, SALT scales to fleet sizes where MARL baselines become intractable and transfers without retraining to fleet sizes unseen during training. In a vehicle-delivery experiment on the southern Manhattan road network, where MARL baselines are computationally prohibitive, SALT improves travel time by 15-21% over a shortest-path routing heuristic. In stochastic grid worlds, SALT reduces average travel times by 23-61% relative to MARL baselines and scales to population sizes where these methods fail.

Authors:Dogan Urgun
Date:2026-10-27 20:11:29

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) enables teams of agents, such as robots and autonomous vehicles, to learn coordinated behavior, but what such teams learn is largely determined by their reward function. Because task success alone provides too little guidance for learning, intermediate rewards are usually designed by hand, and this effort must often be repeated whenever the environment or the team changes. Large language models (LLMs) can automate the effort by writing reward functions as code, yet the resulting rewards are typically designed and tested in a single setting. To enable their reuse across settings, GARDEN (Generalist Autonomous Reward Design through Evidence Navigation) is proposed, in which one reward program is developed for a family of related scenarios and transferred, unchanged, to scenarios that were not available during its design. These results show that a single autonomously engineered reward can be reused effectively across related cooperative scenarios.

Centralized Critics for N-Agent Ad Hoc Teamwork

Authors:Andrew McAlinden, Caroline Wang, Steven Shi, Arrasy Rahman, Elad Liebman, Peter Stone
Date:2026-10-26 03:41:48

Centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) has become the dominant paradigm in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), motivating extensive study of what privileged information should be made available to the critic during training. While prior work has examined the role of global state in fully cooperative settings, the design of centralized critics for N-agent ad hoc teamwork - in which dynamically varying set of learning agents must coordinate with an arbitrary set of uncontrolled teammates drawn from an unknown distribution - remains poorly understood. We study this question by comparing two natural classes of advantage estimators: controlled history estimators, which condition only on the observation and action histories of the learner's own agents, and joint history estimators, which additionally condition on the histories of the uncontrolled teammates. We prove that both estimators are unbiased and characterize the conditions under which the joint history estimate attains strictly lower variance than its controlled counterpart. Empirically, joint history critics match or outperform controlled history critics across a range of NAHT tasks, yielding improved sample efficiency and final returns. Together, these results provide both a theoretical justification and an empirical recommendation for incorporating uncontrolled agent histories into centralized critics for N-agent ad hoc teamwork.
