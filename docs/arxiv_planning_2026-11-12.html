A first application of split conformal prediction for dose prediction in knowledge-based radiotherapy planning

Authors:Raphaël Brodeur, Anne-Sophie Charest, Louis Archambault
Date:2026-11-10 18:10:47

Radiation therapy treatment planning is a labour-intensive process prone to inter-expert variability. Deep learning (DL) dose prediction can accelerate this process, but most DL models do not quantify their reliability. In this study, we construct prediction intervals for DL-based radiotherapy dose prediction using split conformal prediction (CP), a model-agnostic and distribution-free method for uncertainty quantification that provides finite-sample coverage guarantees. We propose patient-level nonconformity scores obtained by aggregating voxelwise scores. Specifically, we formulate a maximum aggregation construction, which guarantees simultaneous coverage of the true dose in all voxels of a future patient with at least a user-defined probability, and a quantile-based construction, which guarantees, with the same probability lower bound, that a future patient attains a prescribed minimum level of voxel coverage. We also investigate groupwise calibration for region-specific guarantees and predictive uncertainty scaling for locally adaptive prediction intervals. The methods are evaluated on head-and-neck cases using a deep ensemble model. The proposed constructions achieved empirical coverage consistent with the nominal levels. Maximum aggregation led to conservative prediction intervals, whereas quantile aggregation produced narrower intervals. Groupwise calibration and uncertainty scaling reduced interval widths in some anatomical regions, while providing region-specific guarantees and local adaptivity. This is the first application of split CP to radiotherapy dose prediction. The explicit patient-level coverage guarantees offer a more interpretable characterisation of prediction reliability than raw uncertainty estimates. Split CP could help clinicians assess the reliability of model predictions and identify cases requiring further review.

Earlybird: Predictive Streaming Speech Recognition in 29M Parameters

Authors:Sahil Mahendrakar
Date:2026-11-10 17:59:28

Streaming speech recognizers output a word only after the speaker has finished it, typically 90-400 ms later. For a voice agent every millisecond counts: the sooner the words are outputted, the sooner the agent can start a search or plan its reply. We present Earlybird, a 29M-parameter streaming recognizer (16.8 MB at 4 bits). Earlybird learns from audio cut off at random points, including partway through a word, and is asked to predict every word that is due by then in full, plus a guess of the next two words. Its raw token probabilities give a confidence for every word including the one still being spoken. On clean audiobook speech (LibriSpeech test-clean), the median word is predicted correctly 90 ms before the speaker finishes it, while released streaming models get it 90 to 400 ms after it ends. Earlybird is the earliest on all six test sets we time, covering audiobooks, TED talks, public speech, earnings calls, and synthetic speech. On the same audiobooks, 69% of words are predicted correctly before they end, against 3-25% for the other models. For words still being spoken, confidence is calibrated, with an expected calibration error (ECE) of 3.7 to 5.5. On the eight English Open ASR Leaderboard sets, it is more accurate than Moonshine Tiny on every set and roughly ties Whistle, a model of the same size.

Authors:Bo Dai, Yilun Du
Date:2026-11-10 17:37:22

Energy-based models (EBMs) define a distribution through a scalar energy, $p_θ(x)\propto\exp(-E_θ(x))$, and are usually presented as one model family among many. This tutorial has a broader goal: beyond introducing EBMs, it uses them as a single lens that connects the different aspects of machine learning. Any EBM is specified by three choices, the energy, which determines what the model represents; the inference procedure, which samples from or optimizes the energy; and the learning method, which fits the energy to data. We first develop these components, including sampling, variational inference, and learning by maximum likelihood, noise contrastive estimation, pseudolikelihood, score matching, and adversarial training, and show that the learning methods share one contrastive structure. This view explains existing methods, organizes them into a common framework, and suggests new algorithms by replacing one component while keeping the others, with composition of energies as a principled way to combine learned models at inference time.
