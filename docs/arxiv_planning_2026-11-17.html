How VLMs Plan What's Next

Authors:Rohit Gandikota, Arnab Sen Sharma, Adam Belfki, David Bau
Date:2026-11-16 18:58:30

When a vision-language model describes an image, it names one object at a time and it rarely repeats itself, yet how the model plans this order is far from obvious. We characterize the mechanism a VLM uses to decide where to look before it looks. The mechanism has four main parts. First, binding heads match the relevant text tokens against the image and resolve them into pointers to the matched image region. Second, the same binding heads bind each written answer to the region it came from, and the model turns it into a progress signal: a cursor, the position in the image up to which the list has been written. Third, a select step chooses the nearest unwritten region after the cursor, or stops. Finally, gaze heads execute the chosen pointer and return a concept of what is drawn there, which language heads and the late MLPs turn into a word in the language of the question. We validate each part of this mechanism through causal patching in Qwen3-VL models on a synthetic visual retrieval task, and test our mechanisms against alternative hypotheses. We further show that the mechanism holds across input formats, natural photographs, and languages, and that its binding, relay, and gaze heads appear at every model size from 2B to 32B. This work allows us to investigate how a VLM turns a question and an image into a sequence of answers, layer by layer and token by token. More broadly, it opens a way to study how models plan over complex multi-step tasks with long rollouts. Our project is available at https://plan.baulab.info/

Wildfire-SSR: Geolocation-Aware Multimodal Fusion for Structure-Level Wildfire Risk Assessment

Authors:Yu-Hsuan Ho, Yiming Xiao, Hao-En Lai, Ali Mostafavi, Samuel D. Brody
Date:2026-11-16 18:40:49

Structure-level wildfire vulnerability assessment is critical for reducing losses in wildland-urban interface communities, yet existing methods often fail to combine parcel-scale ignition pathways with community-scale geographic coverage. This study introduces Wildfire-SSR (Satellite and Street-view for wildfire Risk), a geolocation-aware multimodal framework that integrates macro-scale satellite context with micro-scale street-view evidence. A Vision-Language Model extracts wildfire-relevant building and site attributes from pre-event street-view panoramas and converts them into structured semantic text. These features are spatially aligned with satellite imagery through a decoupled cross-attention fusion decoder, while a frozen satellite-pretrained DINOv3 encoder captures overhead visual context. Alternative geolocation embedding strategies are evaluated to anchor text-derived features within each satellite patch. The model also shows a conservative bias in higher-risk classes, reducing severe-risk underestimation, and completes end-to-end assessment of the study area in 37 minutes on a single GPU. Wildfire-SSR reframes cross-view fusion as localized semantic alignment and provides a scalable tool for mitigation prioritization, resilience planning, and insurance-oriented wildfire risk assessment.

Exploring Low-Regret Energy System Transition Pathways with Modeling to Generate Alternatives

Authors:Hendrik Schricker, Maria Louise Schmitt, Philipp Kleusberg, Valentin Dinges, Benedikt Schuler, Frederike Kuperjans, Boyung Jürgens, Niklas von der Assen
Date:2026-11-16 18:22:52

Long-term planning of energy system transitions is complex due to interdependent investment decisions and uncertain market and policy conditions. Stochastic optimization models account for this uncertainty by identifying a single optimal hedging strategy. However, the flexibility in technology choice and investment timing, i.e., the transition flexibility, usually remains unexplored. Modeling to generate alternatives (MGA) explores near-optimal solution spaces but has not been coupled with stochastic optimization to quantify the transition flexibility. We explore the low-regret pathway space, i.e., all transition pathways that stay within a chosen regret tolerance in every scenario. We introduce a termination criterion for MGA that bounds the exploration error in high-dimensional solution spaces and measure how far each pathway decision, once taken, narrows the capacity ranges of the others. We apply the method to an industrial energy system in Germany. The pathway that minimizes the worst-case regret keeps it at 2.2%, compared with up to 30.9% for perfect-foresight pathways. The transition flexibility increases most at low regret tolerances. At 3.5%, the capacity ranges are on average about 60% as wide as at the highest regret tolerance of 10%. A large share of these capacity ranges is surplus capacity, i.e., capacity installed in addition to, not instead of, other capacity. Surplus capacity is largely not operated but, once installed, narrows the other capacity ranges more strongly than operated capacity. Exploring the low-regret pathway space, rather than reporting a single optimal pathway, therefore is the more informative basis for planning energy system transitions under uncertainty.

Lazy-MOSAIC: Accelerating Skill-Centric Manipulation Planning with Learned Skill-Effect World Models

Authors:Itamar Mishani, Mukund Krishnakumar, Oren Salzman, Maxim Likhachev
Date:2026-11-16 18:10:52

Long-horizon manipulation tasks require a robot to perform a sequence of physical interactions with its environment, typically combining several different skills. Planning such behaviors efficiently is challenging: given a library of parameterized skills, solutions lie in a vast hybrid space of skill sequences and their parameters, and evaluating each candidate skill's feasibility and effect with a high-fidelity world model such as a physics simulator is computationally expensive. MOSAIC, a recent skill-centric approach, lets the skills guide a non-sequential search toward the regions of this space where they are likely to succeed; however, it pays the full cost of simulation for every parameterized skill it considers. In this work, we introduce Lazy-MOSAIC, a lazy planning framework that instead searches over hypothesized skill outcomes. A cheap learned skill-effect world model predicts, in a fraction of a millisecond, a skill's feasibility and the world state it produces. Lazy-MOSAIC then uses these predictions to focus expensive simulation on promising candidate skills and, when a hypothesis is invalidated, repairs only the affected part of the search, while ensuring that every skill in a returned plan is validated in simulation. We evaluate Lazy-MOSAIC on cluttered-shelf retrieval tasks of increasing difficulty and demonstrate it on a physical robot, showing that it plans substantially faster than baselines with fewer simulator queries, while matching or exceeding their success rate in every setting. Randomization protects the average, not the trial: chance covariate imbalance and a calibration of balance $P$ values at the design stage

Authors:William J. Dwyer, David Zurakowski
Date:2026-11-16 17:15:17

Randomization makes the unadjusted treatment-effect estimate unbiased over the allocations a scheme could produce, although it does not balance any single trial. Chance imbalances on strongly prognostic baseline variables directly threaten the validity and regulatory credibility of trial results, yet what design-stage balance $P$ values imply for bias and error rates is seldom stated. We review evidence on chance covariate imbalance and derive that link under a linear model with one normal prognostic covariate, with simulation checks. Given the realized allocation, the unadjusted estimate is biased by $β$ times the difference in covariate means; in standard-error units the bias is $ρZ_{\mathrm{bal}}$, where $ρ$ is the covariate-outcome correlation and $Z_{\mathrm{bal}}$ the balance z-statistic, so it does not shrink as trials grow. Pre-specified ANCOVA removes this bias, change scores reverse it, and non-collapsibility attenuates odds and hazard ratios even under perfect balance. A worst-balance threshold of $P = 1/e$ marks where the Sellke-Bayarri-Berger bound reaches 1; under complete randomization the worst of $k$ independent balance $P$ values falls below it with probability $1-(1-1/e)^k$, 0.99 for $k=10$. Any balance $P$ value $\geq 0.317$ keeps the two-sided conditional type I error of the unadjusted test at or below 5% for every $ρ$; at $1/e$ the one-sided error in the favoured direction is at most 4.1%, against 13-33% at $P = 0.05$. Design-stage balance scores should be benchmarked against complete randomization, weighted by external prognostic strength, and reported alongside allocation predictability and a pre-specified covariate-adjusted analysis plan.

The Price of Not Planning: Parallel Sampling Depth Peaks at the Reconstruction Threshold

Authors:Chuling Wen, Weijie Liang, Jian Lu
Date:2026-11-16 15:54:50

Masked diffusion models sample the tokens of one round independently. Planned decoders first generate a coarse plan, then fill in the tokens in parallel. We ask when a plan saves rounds, how many it saves, and how fine it must be. We study this on broadcast trees: the leaves are the tokens, the internal nodes are the plan, and the edge correlation $θ$ sets how strongly tokens depend on the plan. Errors are measured by the forward Kullback-Leibler divergence $\varepsilon$, and the lower bounds hold for every deterministic value-adaptive rule, also with learned conditionals. We show that a decoder without the plan needs of order $\min\{n,(n/\varepsilon)^α\}$ rounds below the reconstruction threshold, where $α=\log b/(4\log(1/θ))$ rises to $1/2$ at the threshold, and that the exponent stays below $1/2$ above it (binary channel: $0.02\leθ\le0.98$). With the plan, $\log_b n+1$ rounds suffice. In the binary model below the threshold, a plan with units of $m$ tokens needs of order $\log_b(n/m)+\min\{m,(n/\varepsilon)^α\}$ rounds, so it gives logarithmic depth exactly when $m$ is logarithmic. A plan therefore helps most near the threshold, and only if its units are small. Exact divergence computations in the Gaussian model with up to $2^{20}$ leaves confirm these exponents below the threshold.

Date:2026-11-16 03:59:20

We study false discovery rate (FDR) control for a fixed family of hypotheses tested sequentially in a prespecified order. Each decision uses only the current and preceding $p$-values, is not subsequently revised, and permits continued testing after non-rejections. We distinguish generalized fixed-sequence procedures based on preceding rejection counts from fallback-type procedures that additionally use recursively transferred testing levels. For rejection-count procedures, we study a position-dependent calibration under independence and a linear rule calibrated through null marginal distribution functions under positive left-tail dependence under the null. We clarify their connections to existing fixed-sequence and online FDR methods. The fallback construction combines the total number of preceding discoveries with level transfer along consecutive rejections, allowing thresholds to reflect the arrangement of earlier outcomes. We establish its FDR control under history-based positive regression dependence and, with a position-specific correction, under arbitrary dependence. For all four constructions, the guarantees extend to every deterministic testing time within the planned horizon. Gaussian simulations assess the effects of signal placement, dependence, and allocation weights. They demonstrate power gains over selected benchmarks while identifying the conservativeness of the arbitrary-dependence correction.

To design a periodic material, engineers choose the shape and dimensions of a repeating cell to meet target properties. With few simulations, the benefit of a learned predictor can be hard to separate from the effect of which designs the search tries and how it uses simulator feedback. We compare two ways to predict properties in a library of 80 cell architectures, with up to 30 simulator calls per target. For each architecture, both searches use a table of predicted properties at different dimensions. One fills the table with predictions from neural networks. The other fits a simple model to the existing simulations for that architecture. Both searches use the difference between the predicted and simulated properties to adjust the next design. We generated 200 targets from simulated designs after fixing the analysis plan. The two searches showed no detectable difference in how often they matched all three properties within 2% or in the mean error of their returned designs. Designers can use this comparison to judge what a learned predictor adds when the search, feedback and budget are the same.
