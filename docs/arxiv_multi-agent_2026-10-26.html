Cross-Region Cooperation of UAV Swarms Towards Air-Ground ISAC via Hierarchical Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Authors:Linghui Miao, Nhan Thanh Nguyen, Shijian Gao
Date:2026-10-22 10:34:17

The limited communication resources and sensing perspectives of a single uncrewed aerial vehicle (UAV) motivate UAV swarms for integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) in air-ground networks. However, in multi-region scenarios, regional UAV allocations may not match vehicle demand, while local observations may lack sufficient diversity for accurate sensing. To address these issues, this paper proposes a cross-region cooperation framework for UAV swarms. For communication, cross-region UAV reallocation reduces service disparities by matching resources to vehicle demand. For sensing, combining UAV observations from different regions increases observation diversity, while adaptive handshaking guided by marginal Cramér--Rao bound (CRB) utilities balances phase synchronization gains against handshaking energy consumption. Joint optimization of UAV positions and transmit power within each region then further improves communication service and sensing performance. These coupled decisions are coordinated by a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning (H-MARL) approach through slow-timescale inter-region UAV reallocation and fast-timescale intra-region UAV control. Under the considered simulation settings, reallocation can improve quality of service (QoS) by over 20 percentage points compared with retaining the initial allocation under severe resource-demand mismatches. Cooperative sensing with adaptive handshaking incurs only about a 5.0% increase in mean CRB relative to perfect synchronization.

SpecMAGIC: Specification-Guided Multi-Task Generalization in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Authors:Zijian Guo, İlker Işık, H. M. Sabbir Ahmad, Wenchao Li
Date:2026-10-21 20:52:48

Multi-task generalization is an important yet underexplored problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). Many real-world applications require multi-agent systems to generalize across complex, temporally extended tasks with diverse inter-agent coordination requirements. We introduce a specification language that combines temporal-logic task specifications with operators for expressing such requirements. We propose SpecMAGIC, a hierarchical framework that leverages the automaton structure of task specifications for subtask decomposition. A centralized high-level planner identifies and allocates compatible subtask sequences across agents, while decentralized low-level policies execute individual subtasks. One practical form of this is parallel scaling, where a model samples multiple independent reasoning streams and aggregates their answers using methods like voting, reranking, or verification. While effective, this approach leaves the streams uncoordinated, and a discovery made in one rollout cannot influence any other. Multi-Sequence Verifiers jointly score candidate answers after generation, but the reasoning streams remain independent during inference. ParScale takes a step toward model-internal parallel scaling by learning to aggregate multiple forward passes, but its streams still do not exchange information while processing a sequence. Authors:Ziheng Zhang, Wei Zhang, Yunyi Zhao, Zuming Liu, Wei Zhou, Boon Ping Gan
Date:2026-10-15 08:54:27

Flexible job-shop scheduling becomes resource-coupled when a scarce vehicle fleet moves jobs between stations, because the schedule sets the transport waits. BOLT (bound-oriented learning for transport-coupled scheduling) builds a lower bound from chain, machine-capacity, and fleet-capacity relaxations. The bound equals the makespan at termination, so its per-step changes form a reward that sums to a constant minus the makespan. Because it never exceeds the attainable makespan, each candidate's bound increase is a proven rise of the makespan floor, which the policy reads as a price. In the three-vehicle 80-module batch, its mean makespan is 13.0% below the best pair and 4.2% below an hour-budget constraint-programming solver whose proven gap averages 34%. BOLT decides in 2.3-5.9 ms per event on one processor core and re-plans a disruption in seconds.

Authors:Svar Rajankumar Patel, Kshitij Jerath

In cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), agents must learn to coordinate their actions to achieve shared objectives. A key indicator of effective coordination is behavioral diversity, i.e., the agents' potential to enact distinct yet complementary control policies given the same set of observations. Although behavioral diversity can enable role specialization and richer joint behaviors, its appropriate level is highly context- and task-dependent and often costly to tune. We propose Adaptive Diversity Control (ADiCo), a two-timescale framework that adapts behavioral diversity online during training. On a fast timescale, Diversity Control (DiCo) regulates system diversity toward a target value. These results demonstrate that incorporating classical feedback control into MARL training enables effective coordination by providing a systematic alternative to task-specific manual selection of the diversity target.

Position: Beyond Training Time, Test-Time Coordination is Essential for Cooperative MARL

Authors:Dongsu Lee, Sooraj Sathish, Joonkyung Kim, Woojun Kim, Amy Zhang
Date:2026-10-12 21:17:48

We argue that cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) should treat test-time coordination as part of the problem definition rather than a deployment artifact. Our focus is on long-horizon, centrally monitored cooperative systems such as warehouse robotics, automated storage-and-retrieval systems, and cloud-resource scheduling, where teams share a reward, operate under shifting objectives, and already run with global telemetry at deployment. The dominant paradigm in this regime, centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE), uses global information only during training and provides no coordination channel at execution. This is sufficient when agents are weakly coupled or the environment is stationary, but it fails when the team must realign after conditions change. The limitation is structural rather than algorithmic, and more training cannot add a channel that does not exist at execution time. We call this the recoverability gap. To address it, we name the underexplored design space of intermittent test-time coordination as centralized training with mixed execution (CTME), in which a coordinator observes global state and emits high-level signals while agents act locally. CTME is not a particular learning algorithm, but a cooperative MARL problem setting that makes test-time information structure explicit. It specifies team-level information, high-level signals, and update frequency after training, with CTDE recovered as a limit case. We do not argue that CTDE is obsolete. We argue that removing the test-time channel is a modeling choice, not a necessity, and the wrong default when global telemetry is available and teams must recover from drift.

Collective Autonomous Vehicle (AV) route choices in real-world traffic networks can create externalities for Human-Driven Vehicles (HDVs), as routes that benefit AVs may increase travel times for other road users. To address this, we introduce a social component, the marginal cost, to the selfish travel time AV reward. Marginal cost captures an AV's impact on other road users by comparing their travel times with those in a counterfactual simulation in which the AV is removed. We study AV route choice in mixed-autonomy traffic settings using Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), where AVs learn routing policies through repeated interactions with the traffic environment. Experiments on the real-world networks of Ingolstadt and Provins show that incorporating marginal cost into the AV reward not only limits the increase in HDV travel times and shortens AV travel times relative to selfish AV routing, but also improves overall system performance. In Ingolstadt, selfish routing of 40\% AVs increases the travel times of other vehicles by 1.7\%, and this drops to 1.1\% when the social reward is included. Interestingly, not only does overall system performance improve, but the AVs are also better off when acting socially.This effect is consistent across tested cities, penetration rates, and replications, yet when the weight of the social component in the reward increases, the system fails to reach the desired performance. For a more scalable approach to social AV routing in mixed-autonomy traffic, we compute the marginal cost only for the AVs whose route choices most affect the traffic system. This reduces the number of counterfactual simulations and achieves similar performance at a significantly lower computational cost. Our results suggest that AV routing should go beyond selfish rewards and, for the benefit of all traffic participants, include a social component in its objective.
